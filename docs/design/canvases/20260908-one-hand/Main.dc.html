Design canvas · 2026-09-08 · read me first

One hand — why DiveDay feels like four products, and four ways to make it one

The owner's brief: the app is not consistent in voice, spacing, elegance, delight, system and components; look at everything; propose at least three approaches; think creatively and top-down; the current visual style is not sacred. This canvas answers in three parts — a diagnosis read from the running app, an audit sheet that draws the evidence, and four directions that each redraw the same two surfaces for the same shop on the same morning. Nothing here is normative until the owner picks and the ADR moves to Accepted (H-71).

The diagnosis in one paragraph

DiveDay does not lack a design system. It has a good one — tokens at zero drift, one page header on 40 of 47 staff pages, one control class on every input, one empty state, one chrome bar shared by staff and diver pages — and it still feels inconsistent, because five design passes landed in twelve days (Clearwater on 08-27, Reef on 09-01, Reef-all-the-way-down on 09-04, Before-you-ask on 09-06, Nothing-from-nowhere on 09-07) and each one added a vocabulary without deleting the one before. So on a single screen of Today a departure's rows sit in Reef's 28px panel, the desk's rows sit on Clearwater's bare ledger, and the horizons sit in pills that belong to neither. The seams are in the six things a checker cannot see and a page author decides alone every time: the page's width, what the eyebrow says, how a group carries its count, what a row is, how you add a thing, and how a state is drawn. Each of those has between three and nineteen spellings today, and the disclosure beside them has twenty-seven. The voice has the same shape of problem: one rule (a good briefing) and thirty-six bundle authors, so a title is a noun on one page and a question on the next, and a group label joins its count with an em dash on Reviews and a middle dot on Inbox.

Four directions, one fiction

Each board below redraws the shop home and the Gear register for Blue Mantis Divers on Thursday, August 27, 2026 — the same day every canvas since Clearwater has used — so the four compare like for like. Direction A changes nothing but the forks. B, C and D each replace the surface and keep A's deletions underneath. They are deliberately far apart: a tidier Reef, a paper logbook, a dive computer, and a system where the sentences are the components.

A · least change

Finish the sentence

Reef stays. Delete every second spelling, hold each job at one, draw nothing new. The app stops looking like four redesigns because three are finally removed.

For: cheapest, safest, no owner decision on taste, and the floor the others need anyway.

Against: it will look like today, tidier. Nobody says "wow" at a list that lines up.

Cost: 2–3 sessions.

B · a new surface

The logbook

Every surface is a page of the shop's one book: a margin column, ruled entries, a figure or a verb in the right column, a stamp when a thing is settled. No panels, no shadows, no pills, no chevrons.

For: the object every diver already owns; unmistakable in the category; the manifest was always going to be printed.

Against: paper is a light-mode idea; a serif in glare; a fifth look in three weeks.

Cost: 5–7 sessions.

C · a new surface

The instrument

The staff tool looks like the other tool on the deck: a dive computer. Dark by default, one cyan for every action, every number a figure, every state a word in a fixed slot. Two components — a readout and a line — and nothing else.

For: the dock test becomes the brand; crews love it; the shop's own brand carries the warmth on the storefront.

Against: cold at 6 AM at a desk; light mode is second-class; Reef's three moments are re-cut or moved.

Cost: 6–8 sessions.

D · the voice is the system

The briefing

Every surface opens by telling you the state in five sentence shapes, and the controls are verbs inside the sentences. The kind word, the chevron and the right-hand action go; the sentence carries all three.

For: principle 4 made literal; the one kind of consistency a guard can read; the highest "wow" for an owner reading at 6 AM.

Against: prose scans slower than columns; Orders loses its money column; every sentence written well twice.

Cost: 4–6 sessions, mostly copy.

What no direction changes

The name and the bubble mark. Harbor: the diver-facing pages wear the shop's brand, so none of these directions touches the storefront's colours (B, C and D change what the storefront is made of, not whose it is). The dock test — 44px targets, 16px critical text, never colour alone. The coral bans on manifests, roll call, certs, waivers and payments. The claims policy. The i18n rule that every word comes from a bundle. Boat mode, which stays Geist and high-contrast under every direction, including B.

The owner's call — H-71

(a) Which direction: A alone, or A plus one of B, C, D. The recommendation, stated so it can be disagreed with: A now, then D — the floor is two or three sessions of deletion and the guards that keep it deleted; D is the only surface change whose consistency a script can read, it keeps Reef's palette and the hand the owner just chose, and it is the one that a shop owner would describe to another shop owner in a sentence. B is the most distinctive and the most expensive; C is the best on a boat and the worst at a desk.

(b) Whether the page title becomes the tab's word everywhere (Reports, not "How's your month"; Inbox, not "What divers wrote"; Check-in, not "Counter check-in"). Recommended yes: a title that is a sentence is a lede that stole the heading's slot.

(c) Whether the twelve terminal outcomes on the six shared doors (email verified, password reset, party claimed, contact confirmed, review submitted, course completed) get the earned moment the waiver already has. Recommended yes, in one component, so the seam closes at one place.

(d) For B only: whether a second face (Newsreader, or another serif) is permitted on staff pages, reversing the Geist-only pick of 09-01. If no, B is A with rules instead of panels, which is still a direction, but a smaller one.
