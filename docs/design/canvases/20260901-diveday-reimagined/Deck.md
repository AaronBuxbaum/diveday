# Deck — the instrument

**In one sentence:** DiveDay reads like the boat's own instruments — every surface led by a large tabular figure, structured with hairlines instead of boxes, dark at depth by default and sunlit when the reader is standing in daylight.

**Motivation.** A dive shop's questions are all numbers: how many are booked, how many can't board, how long until the lines come off, how many came back. Today those numbers are sentences with figures embedded in them, and a staffer has to *read* to find out whether the day is fine. Deck inverts that — the number is the interface, the words are the caption — so the answer arrives before the reading does. It also takes the dock test seriously as an aesthetic rather than a checklist: a dive computer, a depth gauge and a Braun console are all dense, high-contrast, unornamented instruments, and that is exactly the register a wet 6 AM deck asks for.

**The honest tradeoff.** Deck is colder than DiveDay has ever been. The brand's own words are "gentle and nice," and an instrument is not gentle — a shop owner who loves the warm sand palette may find this severe, and a diver landing on a dark storefront could read a nightclub rather than a dive shop (which is why the storefront is sunlit; see below). It also spends its budget on precision, so it has almost no charm of its own: everything delightful in Deck has to come from the shop's data being *right*, and there is nowhere to hide a mediocre moment behind a nice illustration.

**Three wow moments.**
1. **The readout** — one figure per surface, at 54–76px in the data face, that *is* the answer: 10/12 aboard, 5 seats left, 9/10 back. It is also the only place coral is spent: the instant a count reaches parity, the figure turns coral and says "Everyone is on the boat" in words beside it.
2. **T−minus** — the day is a countdown ladder, not a calendar. Every departure carries the time left until it sails and the number of people who cannot board yet, so the order of the morning's work is decided before a single sentence has been read.
3. **Roll call at depth** — 56px cells, a tap that undoes itself, a count that will not round up, and a scheme that doesn't blind a crew on a night dive. It is the screen that proves a booking engine's passenger list is not a manifest, and it is the one we put in front of a FareHarbor shop.

**Kept from Clearwater:** hairline structure over card stacks, group headers owning shared facts, tabular figures, icon-plus-word states, coral rationed to earned moments, flat-at-rest elevation, and the ban on colour-only status.
**Thrown out:** the sunlit-sand default (Depth leads now), the 12–14px working type (16px is the floor for anything a person acts on), soft 12–14px radii (0/3/6 only), the timeline spine with its dots and connectors, and any progress bar that colours the achievement rather than the gap.

**Faces.** **Geist** for words — headings, rows, prose — keeping DiveDay's voice; **IBM Plex Mono** for every figure, label and countdown, because it was drawn for engineering documents, holds its numerals at 11px and at 76px, and gives times, seats and money a shared grid without a table. Fallbacks: `system-ui, sans-serif` and `ui-monospace, SF Mono, Menlo, monospace`.

**Storefront scheme: sunlit.** The staff app is Deck's home ground and stays dark — a shop reads it at 6 AM on a dock and 11 PM on a night boat. The storefront is a different reader in a different place: a diver in daylight, on a phone, deciding whether these people look like they know what they are doing. A dark public page in sun reads as a nightclub and mirrors badly; sand reads as a dive shop. It is the same structure and the same figures, retuned — which is the argument for the two-scheme system rather than a dark skin.
